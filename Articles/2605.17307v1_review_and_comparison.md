Review of arXiv 2605.17307v1 (Kashif & Slepaczuk, May 2026) and comparison with the FinRL-Tutorials/PortfolioAllocation_paolo_impruvements pipeline
================================================================================================================================================

Paper full title: "Deep Reinforcement Learning Framework for Diversified Portfolio Management Across Global Equity Markets"
Authors: Kamil Kashif and Robert Slepaczuk, University of Warsaw
67 pages, 16 OOS (Out-Of-Sample) walk-forward folds, period 2003-01-02 to 2026-03-13.


1. SUMMARY OF THE PAPER'S APPROACH
----------------------------------

Algorithm
- Soft Actor-Critic (SAC), off-policy, twin critics, replay buffer, soft target updates.
- Entropy coefficient alpha fixed at 0.2 (NOT auto-tuned). The authors explicitly state that adaptive alpha was unstable across walk-forward folds because of financial-data non-stationarity and short validation windows. Fixed value gives consistent exploration-exploitation across folds.

Policy parameterisation
- Output is the parameters of a Dirichlet distribution, from which the weight vector is sampled. This guarantees long-only, non-negative weights summing to 1 by construction (no post-hoc projection needed).
- Two policy structures compared:
  a. FLAT Dirichlet: single distribution over (N assets + optional cash).
  b. HIERARCHICAL Dirichlet: first samples the equity-vs-cash split, then samples weights within equity from a second Dirichlet.

Encoder
- Two alternatives:
  a. LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory) applied per-asset over a 60-day lookback, embeddings then combined with cross-sectional attention.
  b. Transformer (2-layer self-attention, hidden 128) over the same window.
- The MLP (Multi-Layer Perceptron)-only encoder used in our project is NOT in their comparison set.

State representation
Per-asset features computed every day:
  - Momentum log-returns at horizons {1, 5, 20, 60} days
  - Rolling std at {5, 20} days
  - Technical indicators: RSI (Relative Strength Index), MACD (Moving Average Convergence Divergence) histogram, Bollinger %B, distance from 20-day high, 20-day mean-reversion deviation
  - 60-day rolling beta vs market proxy ETF (Exchange-Traded Fund)
  - 20-day log-return (absolute performance feature)
Global features (same for all assets, broadcast across asset axis):
  - VIX (Volatility Index, CBOE - Chicago Board Options Exchange) level and 5-day change
  - Cross-sectional average return + 5-day rolling vol
  - Market breadth = % of assets with positive medium-term return
  - Market-proxy ETF returns over 5- and 20-day horizons
Pre-filtering: a TOP-K cross-sectional momentum filter (k = 10/20/30 depending on config) selects the k assets with highest 120-day momentum at each rebalance date BEFORE the agent sees the state. This caps action-space dimensionality and keeps the state size fixed despite varying index composition.

Action space
- Vector of (N + 1) weights = N risky assets + 1 cash position (when cash is allowed).
- Long-only, sum to 1.
- Cash dimension is dropped in the "fully invested" configurations.

Reward function
Two variants:
  Absolute:          r_t = 1000 * log(1 + r_net) - lambda_TO * TO_t * 100 - lambda_conc * (HHI_t - 1/N) * 100
  Benchmark-relative: r_t = 1000 * (log(1+r_net) - log(1+r_benchmark)) - lambda_TO * TO_t * 100 - lambda_conc * (HHI_t - 1/N) * 100
Where:
  - r_net is the portfolio return net of transaction costs (2 bps - basis points - per unit turnover).
  - TO_t (Turnover at time t) = L1 (Manhattan / sum-of-absolute) distance between consecutive weight vectors.
  - HHI_t (Herfindahl-Hirschman Index at time t) = sum(w_i^2), a measure of concentration. Penalising HHI - 1/N pushes the policy toward equal-weight diversification.
  - lambda_TO = 0.003, lambda_conc in {0.0, 0.1, 0.5} depending on config.

Five configurations compared
  LSTM_1:        LSTM encoder, flat Dirichlet, log-return reward, cash allowed, k=20/30
  LSTM_2:        LSTM encoder, hierarchical Dirichlet, log-return reward, cash allowed, k=20/30
  LSTM_NC_1:     LSTM encoder, flat Dirichlet, benchmark-relative reward, fully invested (NC = No Cash), k=10/20
  LSTM_NC_2:     LSTM encoder, flat Dirichlet, benchmark-relative reward, fully invested, k=20
  TRANSFORMERS:  Transformer encoder, flat Dirichlet, log-return reward, cash allowed, k=20/30

Training and evaluation
- Walk-forward with rolling (non-anchored) windows: 5 years train + 1 year validation + 1 year test, stepping forward by 1 year. 16 OOS folds covering 2009-04 to 2026-03.
- Adaptive retraining: at each fold, the model is retrained ONLY IF validation Sharpe falls below median(last 5) - 0.5*std(last 5), OR validation Sharpe is negative, OR 3 consecutive folds have been skipped. Reduces compute when the previous model is still good.
- Hyperparameter selection: tiered. Prefer configs that perform consistently on BOTH train and val. Among those, prefer minimal train/val gap. Only if no consistency match exists, take the highest validation Sharpe.

Other key training settings
- Learning rates: 3e-4 (actor), 5e-4 (critic), 3e-4 (entropy).
- gamma = 0.99, tau = 0.005, batch 128, replay buffer 20,000, warm-up 500, gradient steps per env step = 2.
- 50 epochs, ES (Early Stopping) patience 8.
- Transaction cost: 2 bps. This is the IBKR (Interactive Brokers) institutional/high-volume tier; below the retail commission base.

Compute
- LSTM: ~14 hours per walk-forward cycle on NVIDIA L4 GPU (Graphics Processing Unit) (24GB).
- Transformer: ~23 hours per cycle.
- 16 folds x 3 markets x 5 configs => order of magnitude 1000+ GPU-hours total.

Data
- Three markets: NASDAQ-100, Nikkei 225, EURO STOXX 50.
- Daily prices from yfinance.
- Historical index membership from Bloomberg Terminal: this is what enables survivorship-bias correction. Each constituent's join/leave dates expand to a daily tradability mask.
- Benchmark ETFs: QQQ, EWJ, FEZ.
- Benchmark strategies: Buy & Hold of the benchmark ETF, Equal-Weight Monthly on the top-k momentum universe, Markowitz Minimum Variance (5-year rolling cov, monthly rebal), Momentum Top-20.

Statistical inference
- HAC (Heteroskedasticity- and Autocorrelation-Consistent)-adjusted Newey-West standard errors for mean return differences.
- Stationary bootstrap p-values for Sharpe and IR2 differences.
- Regression of strategy returns on benchmark returns, testing whether alpha > 0 with HAC standard errors.

Headline results

Two metrics are reported throughout. IR1 (Information Ratio = ARC/ASD, i.e. annualised return over annualised volatility) is the basic risk-adjusted metric. IR2 (Modified Information Ratio = IR1 x ARC / MD, i.e. IR1 further penalised by maximum drawdown) is the paper's primary criterion. B&H = Buy & Hold; RL = Reinforcement Learning; EW = Equal-Weight Monthly; Min-Var = Markowitz Minimum Variance. Higher is better on both ratios.

Single-market results (cells are IR2 unless otherwise noted; bold = best per row):

| Market        | B&H IR2  | EW IR2 | Min-Var IR2 | Best RL (IR2)    | Row winner       | Statistical significance at 10%        |
|---------------|----------|--------|-------------|------------------|------------------|----------------------------------------|
| NASDAQ-100    | **0.52** | 0.49   | 0.47        | LSTM_2: 0.46     | B&H              | none                                   |
| Nikkei 225    | 0.018    | 0.19   | 0.17        | LSTM_1: 0.15     | **EW (0.19)**    | none                                   |
| EURO STOXX 50 | 0.06     | 0.07   | 0.06        | LSTM_2: **0.15** | LSTM_2 (RL)      | LSTM_1, LSTM_2, LSTM_NC_2, Transformer |

Notes specific to the single-market table:
- Nikkei 225: Markowitz IR1 = 0.61 is the highest IR1 across all strategies on that market, but it doesn't propagate into IR2 because Markowitz takes higher drawdowns. The IR2 table thus shows EW (Equal-Weight Monthly) winning.
- Nikkei 225 B&H IR2 = 0.018 is essentially zero - dragged down by an 11-year MLD (Maximum Loss Duration) following the 2008 crash, which inflates the denominator of IR2.
- EURO STOXX 50: this is the ONLY market where any RL strategy beats both Buy & Hold AND the classical benchmarks on IR2, and the only market with statistically significant regression alpha at the 10% level (4 of the 5 RL configs).
- Statistical significance refers to the Newey-West HAC-adjusted regression of strategy returns on benchmark returns testing whether alpha > 0. None of the markets show significance under the stricter HAC mean-difference test or the bootstrap test of Sharpe / IR2 differences.

Cross-market ensemble (separate experiment - equal-weight average of strategy returns across the three markets, compared to an equal-weight average of the three markets' B&H benchmarks):

| Strategy    | IR2      | vs common benchmark | Significance (regression alpha) |
|-------------|----------|---------------------|---------------------------------|
| B&H avg     | 0.34     | -                   | -                               |
| LSTM_1      | **0.41** | +0.07               | significant at 10%              |
| LSTM_2      | 0.40     | +0.06               | significant at 10%              |
| LSTM_NC_1   | 0.35     | +0.01               | not significant                 |
| LSTM_NC_2   | 0.35     | +0.01               | not significant                 |
| Transformer | 0.36     | +0.02               | not significant                 |

The cross-market ensemble is the paper's strongest result: LSTM_1 lifts IR2 from 0.34 (passive benchmark) to 0.41, and the regression alpha for LSTM_1 and LSTM_2 reaches 10% significance.

Context for the IR2 numbers
- IR2 in the 0.4-0.5 range corresponds to a Sharpe of roughly 0.6-0.9 (the exact mapping depends on drawdown).
- That is competitive but not extraordinary. Active managers typically aim for Sharpe >= 1.0.
- The "RL wins EURO STOXX 50" headline is real but narrow: even there, LSTM_2's IR2 of 0.15 means the Sharpe is around 0.5, with a fairly high maximum drawdown.

Headline conclusion
"The central hypothesis is only partially confirmed: no strategy achieves statistically significant excess returns relative to Buy & Hold under HAC-robust inference across all markets". RL adds value in non-trending, uncertain markets (EuroStoxx, post-2020 Nikkei). RL is dominated by passive in strongly trending markets (NASDAQ 2014-2019).

Research questions and answers (from section 9)
- RQ1 (Research Question 1) reward formulation: limited impact. Benchmark-relative not consistently better.
- RQ2 policy structure: hierarchical Dirichlet beats flat on risk-adjusted metrics across all 3 markets (lower vol - volatility, lower drawdown).
- RQ3 cash vs no-cash: cash allowed gives lower drawdowns and higher IR2 even though absolute returns are lower.
- RQ4 LSTM vs Transformer: LSTM wins on risk-adjusted metrics consistently.
- RQ5 cross-market ensemble: helps unambiguously. Reduces vol and DD (Drawdown), raises IR2.
- RQ6 regime dependence: RL adds most value in high-uncertainty/low-trend periods; passive dominates in strong trends.

Honest limitations the authors call out
- Configurations are NOT ceteris paribus (encoder, reward, constraint all co-vary), so RQ1-RQ4 are exploratory not causal.
- Transaction cost held fixed at 2 bps throughout. Robustness to retail-level costs (5-10 bps) not tested.
- Walk-forward architecture (5y/1y/1y) not subject to sensitivity analysis - meta-overfitting risk per Bailey et al 2017.
- Ensemble uses fixed equal weights across markets, not optimised.


2. COMMENTARY ON THE PAPER
--------------------------

What's strong
- Multi-market evaluation under identical methodology is rare in this literature; lets the authors isolate "market trendiness" as the dominant factor that determines whether RL helps.
- Survivorship correction via Bloomberg membership data is the right way to do this. Most academic papers skip this and get inflated numbers.
- HAC + bootstrap inference is properly applied; the paper does NOT oversell its results. The conclusion that "RL is only marginally better than passive in 1 of 3 markets" is rigorous.
- Adaptive retraining is a sensible compute-saving heuristic.
- Hierarchical Dirichlet for separating cash-vs-equity from within-equity allocation is a clean idea.
- The regime decomposition (post-GFC - Global Financial Crisis of 2008-2009 / bull market / COVID - Coronavirus Disease 2019 + interest rate hikes) is a useful framing that reveals WHEN RL adds value.

What's weak
- Configurations vary multiple things at once. LSTM_1 vs LSTM_NC_1 differ in encoder seed... no actually they share encoder but differ in reward AND constraint AND top-k (top-k = the cross-sectional top k assets ranked by 120-day momentum). That's three things changing. You can't attribute the difference to any one. The authors themselves admit this.
- alpha fixed at 0.2 is a workaround, not a fix. The instability of auto-tuned alpha hints that SAC is poorly suited to non-stationary financial returns. This is consistent with my own experience: SAC is sensitive in financial RL contexts.
- 14-23 hours per walk-forward cycle on an L4 GPU is a lot of compute for a strategy that only meaningfully beats passive in 1 of 3 markets. Cost-benefit is poor.
- Top-k pre-filter is fine when N=100+. The authors don't ablate it. For N < 20 (our case), top-k is irrelevant.
- No-cash configurations use a BENCHMARK-RELATIVE reward. Cash configurations use ABSOLUTE log-return reward. So reward and cash-availability co-vary perfectly. Whatever effect "cash allowed" has is contaminated by reward choice.
- The "single statistical significance" finding (only EuroStoxx, only via regression alpha, only at 10% level) is, frankly, fragile. Across 5 configs x 3 markets x 2 test methods, you'd expect SOME 10% significance just by chance with no real effect.

How honest is the paper?
- Quite honest. It states clearly that RL doesn't generally beat passive. It doesn't hype the EuroStoxx result.
- The conclusion section explicitly lists 5 limitations including meta-overfitting risk. That's better than typical.

Where the two approaches AGREE
- Walk-forward as the validation framework.
- Long-only weights summing to 1 (no shorts, no leverage).
- Transaction-cost-aware reward.
- ES as the training-stop criterion.
- Buy & Hold and Min-Var as benchmarks (our pipeline also computes these).

Where they DIVERGE materially
- Algorithm choice (PPO vs SAC). Both work; PPO is simpler and more stable in non-stationary settings, which matches the paper's own admission about SAC's alpha instability.
- Universe scale and structure. Our 9 ETFs vs their 100-225 single stocks are fundamentally different problems. Our universe is small enough that top-k filtering is irrelevant. Theirs is large enough that the agent can't reasonably output weights over 100+ assets without it.
- Cash. They have it as an explicit weight; we have it implicit (the agent can rotate into SHY/LQD/TLT, which are bond ETFs and act as quasi-cash, but the agent is forced to hold SOMETHING).
- Temporal modeling. They use LSTM/Transformer to process a 60-day window of features; we use precomputed 252-day rolling covariance as the only "memory" the agent sees.
- Statistical inference. They report HAC p-values. We report informal Sharpe comparisons.


4. PROS AND CONS OF ADOPTING EACH PAPER ELEMENT IN OUR PROJECT
--------------------------------------------------------------

4.1 Switch PPO -> SAC
  Pros: Off-policy is more sample-efficient. Twin critics reduce overestimation. Authors used it successfully.
  Cons: Authors themselves had to disable adaptive alpha because of instability on financial data. Adds replay buffer machinery and many more hyperparameters. Risk of regression in stability.
  Verdict: NOT WORTH IT. Our PPO setup is working and reproducible. Switching is high-risk for low expected gain.

4.2 Switch softmax-projected Gaussian -> Dirichlet
  Pros: Cleaner, native simplex parameterisation. The softmax projection has known issues (gradient flow through softmax is poorer than through a Dirichlet's log-prob).
  Cons: Requires re-implementing the policy network and the env's action handling. Effect is likely small; both produce valid weights on the simplex.
  Verdict: MARGINAL. Useful experiment but not high priority. Maybe 2-3 days of engineering.

4.3 Add LSTM or Transformer encoder
  Pros: Captures temporal patterns the MLP misses (e.g., momentum decay, mean reversion). May help in regime transitions.
  Cons: Big compute increase (paper reports 14-23h per cycle). Currently we run 8 seeds in 7 minutes; adding LSTM could push that to hours. Our universe is small (9 ETFs) so the dimensionality gain is also small.
  Verdict: NOT WORTH IT FOR PRODUCTION. Possibly worth as a research experiment if you want to understand whether temporal encoding helps on the ETF universe.

4.4 Add global features (VIX, market breadth, cross-sectional avg)
  Pros: Low engineering cost (add 4-6 new state dimensions). Likely high information value - VIX is a textbook risk indicator and is publicly available. Market breadth is computable from the 9 tickers we already have.
  Cons: State dimension grows. Negligible compute impact.
  Verdict: HIGH VALUE, LOW COST. Strong recommend. This is the single most actionable thing from the paper.

4.5 Add explicit cash position to the action space
  Pros: Cleaner risk control. The agent can rotate to cash during stress without relying on the binary turbulence gate. Paper's RQ3 confirms cash-allowed beats fully-invested on IR2 across all markets.
  Cons: Requires non-trivial env modification - need to track cash separately, handle cash returns (zero), and ensure the simplex constraint covers N+1 dimensions. May invalidate prior walk-forward validation.
  Verdict: MEDIUM-HIGH VALUE. Worth doing IF you're willing to re-validate the walk-forward. Would also let you retire the explicit risk-off gate (the agent learns when to go to cash by itself).

4.6 Hierarchical Dirichlet policy (cash-vs-equity, then within-equity)
  Pros: Paper says it's the single biggest improvement to risk-adjusted performance. Cleaner decomposition of decisions.
  Cons: Only meaningful if cash is added (4.5). Requires custom policy network. Non-trivial.
  Verdict: PAIR WITH 4.5. If you add cash, this is the natural next step. If you don't add cash, skip.

4.7 Add HHI concentration penalty to the reward
  Pros: Cheap. One line in the env's step(). Discourages winner-take-all allocations. Complements TC penalty.
  Cons: Adds another hyperparameter (lambda_conc) to tune. May fight against the agent finding genuine concentrated bets.
  Verdict: WORTH TRYING. Cheap experiment. Set lambda_conc = 0.1 initially, A/B (split-test) against current setup.

4.8 Top-k momentum pre-filter
  Pros: Reduces action-space dimensionality in large universes.
  Cons: Our universe has 9 tickers. Top-k filtering would just be a no-op or actively harmful.
  Verdict: NOT APPLICABLE.

4.9 Adaptive retraining (skip-when-stable)
  Pros: Saves compute by not retraining when the previous model is still good on validation.
  Cons: Our retraining takes 7 minutes. Saving a few of those isn't material. Adds bookkeeping complexity.
  Verdict: NOT WORTH IT AT OUR SCALE. Useful when training takes hours (their case).

4.10 HAC + bootstrap + regression alpha statistical inference
  Pros: Honest, rigorous assessment of whether the strategy actually beats passive. Currently we eyeball Sharpe differences. This would tell us whether those differences are statistically real.
  Cons: Adds a post-processing step. ~50-100 LOC (Lines Of Code) of statsmodels boilerplate.
  Verdict: HIGH VALUE FOR SCIENCE/HONESTY. Strong recommend. Easy to add as a stage-6 script. Would also help calibrate the kill-switch tripwires in USAGE_LIVE_TRADING.md.

4.11 Cross-market ensemble (NASDAQ + Nikkei + EuroStoxx)
  Pros: The paper's strongest finding. Cross-geography diversification reduces vol and DD even when individual market RL is mediocre.
  Cons: Requires building parallel pipelines for 2 more markets. Each market needs its own data fetch, training, and validation. Non-trivial.
  Verdict: WORTH THE EFFORT IF YOU WANT TO SCALE THE STRATEGY. Probably 1-2 weeks of engineering. The payoff is concrete: an equal-weight ensemble of 3 markets with IR2 0.41 vs single-market 0.15-0.46.

4.12 Survivorship-corrected universe via Bloomberg membership
  Pros: Honest data.
  Cons: Requires a Bloomberg subscription. Doesn't apply to our ETF universe anyway (ETFs are stable).
  Verdict: NOT APPLICABLE.


5. PRIORITIZED IMPLEMENTATION RECOMMENDATION
--------------------------------------------

If you decide to invest engineering time in adopting paper ideas, here's the priority order I would pick. Each item assumes the previous ones are done.

PRIORITY 1: Global features (4.4) and HAC/bootstrap inference (4.10)
- Add VIX level + change, market breadth, cross-sectional average return to the state.
- Add a stage-6 script that computes HAC-adjusted p-values for our PPO ensemble vs DJIA, Min-Var, and Equal-Weight benchmarks.
- Combined effort: 2-3 days. Both are low risk, high information value.
- Outcome: a richer state and a defensible statement about whether our strategy actually has alpha.

PRIORITY 2: Cash position + retire the binary risk-off gate (4.5)
- Add a 10th action dimension for cash. Cash earns zero (or a short-rate proxy).
- Remove or downgrade the turbulence gate to a soft signal.
- Effort: 4-5 days including re-validating walk-forward.
- Outcome: cleaner risk control architecture.

PRIORITY 3: HHI concentration penalty (4.7)
- One line in env.step(). Add lambda_conc to config_production.json.
- A/B test against current setup.
- Effort: 1-2 days including the A/B.
- Outcome: confirm or rule out whether the penalty helps.

PRIORITY 4: Cross-market ensemble (4.11)
- Build parallel pipelines for, say, EuroStoxx ETFs and Japanese ETFs.
- Train each market's PPO ensemble; combine equal-weighted at inference.
- Effort: 1-2 weeks.
- Outcome: actual geographic diversification a la the paper's RQ5.

PRIORITY 5: Optionally Dirichlet policy (4.2) and hierarchical decomposition (4.6)
- Only worth doing AFTER cash is added (priority 2).
- Effort: 1 week.
- Outcome: cleaner policy structure but possibly small empirical gain.

Things I would NOT do
- Switch to SAC (4.1). PPO works; SAC's stability issues on financial data are well documented.
- Add LSTM/Transformer (4.3). 9 ETFs do not need temporal encoding; cov + indicators already summarise sufficient history.
- Top-k filter (4.8). Universe too small.
- Adaptive retraining (4.9). Training too fast at our scale to matter.
- Survivorship correction (4.12). ETFs do not need it.


6. BOTTOM LINE: IS IT WORTH IMPLEMENTING THE PAPER'S APPROACH?
--------------------------------------------------------------

Not as a wholesale replacement. The paper's approach is a research framework optimised for academic comparison across 3 markets and 5 configurations. Our pipeline is optimised for being deployable on a personal trading account.

The paper's headline result is "RL doesn't beat passive in 2 of 3 markets, and only marginally in the third under weak statistical evidence". That's a sobering finding. It suggests that the marginal gain from any RL-based portfolio approach over Buy & Hold or Equal-Weight is modest at best, and probably regime-dependent.

However, the paper has SPECIFIC ELEMENTS worth porting:
1. Global state features (VIX, breadth, market-relative). Cheap, likely useful.
2. Statistical inference (HAC, bootstrap, alpha regression). Essential for honest assessment.
3. Explicit cash position. Cleaner risk control than our binary gate.
4. Concentration penalty (HHI). Cheap experiment.
5. Cross-market ensemble. The paper's strongest empirical finding.

What I would NOT port:
- SAC, Dirichlet, LSTM/Transformer, top-k, adaptive retraining, Bloomberg membership data.

The total work to bring in items 1-4 is roughly 2-3 weeks. Item 5 is another 1-2 weeks if you want geographic diversification.

If the goal is "make our pipeline more honest and defensible" then items 1 and 2 alone are the right investment - they're cheap, they don't disturb the working PPO setup, and they answer the most important question: does our strategy actually have statistically significant alpha relative to passive benchmarks? Right now we don't know with rigour. The paper shows that even sophisticated multi-encoder SAC frameworks usually don't.

That asymmetry - cheap-to-validate, hard-to-make-better - is the practical takeaway from reading this paper carefully.
